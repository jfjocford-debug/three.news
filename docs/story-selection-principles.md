# Story Selection: Core Principles

This governs what gets chosen for a drop, before anyone writes a headline or picks artwork. It sits upstream of the voice and template docs — a well-written deadpan headline can't fix a story that shouldn't have been picked.

## The Core Problem This Solves

Missouri's congressional map ruling and Google hitting $2 trillion are both genuinely significant. Neither has anything a reader can hold onto — no image, no irony, no human stake that lands in one sentence. They're informationally correct and experientially inert. Meanwhile, "the Pope is skipping treatment to keep working" is a smaller story in raw impact terms, but it has a real shape: a specific person, a specific choice, a tension a reader immediately understands.

The lesson isn't "pick less important stories." It's that **importance and narrative shape are two different qualities, and a drop needs stories that have both** — not stories chosen for one while assuming the other will follow from good writing.

## The Two-Question Test

Every candidate story gets evaluated on two independent axes. A story doesn't need to max out both, but it needs a real answer to both, not just one.

### 1. Does it matter?
- Who is actually affected, and how many people?
- Is this new information, or an update to something already understood?
- Would a reasonable person's understanding of the world be measurably better for knowing this?

This is the "essential" half of the promise — the reason this isn't just entertainment.

### 2. Does it have a story?
- Is there a specific person, image, or moment a reader can picture — not just a policy or a number?
- Is there tension, irony, juxtaposition, or a twist — something that isn't just "X happened," but "X happened, which is strange/telling/unexpected because Y"?
- Could you describe it to a friend in one sentence and have them want to know more, not just nod?

This is the half that was missing. A story can matter enormously and still fail this test — a procedural ruling, a quarterly earnings number, a routine vote. Those aren't wrong to know; they're wrong for *this format*, which only gets three shots a day and needs each one to earn its place.

## A Third Lens: Cultural Currency

Specific to this audience, not universal to news generally: **would a person with taste want to be the one who mentions this?** Not "is this shareable" in a viral sense — closer to "does knowing this make someone feel more attuned to what's actually going on, in a way they'd bring up unprompted." This is the filter that protects the "if you know, you know" positioning from drifting into either dry wire-service tone (technically informative, forgettable) or tabloid novelty (memorable, cheapens the brand).

## The Portfolio Rule

Evaluate the three stories in a drop together, not just individually. A drop where all three score high on "matters" and low on "has a story" reads like homework. A drop where all three lean hard into irony/twist reads like a novelty account, not a news product. Aim for range across a drop — at least one story should carry real institutional weight, at least one should have a genuine narrative hook, and ideally one does both.

## What This Does Not Change

- **Story selection happens before headline writing, always.** If a story is being chosen because it would make a good headline, that's the selection process failing, not succeeding — this was true before this doc and stays true now.
- **The voice exception rule is untouched.** Death, violence, child harm, and active criminal proceedings involving real people still get the plain register, regardless of how strong the narrative hook is. A story can pass story selection on the strength of its irony and still be written flat, because selection and voice are separate gates. (The enchilada-argument shooting is the clean example: it would likely pass the "has a story" test easily — real juxtaposition, real stakes — and would still be written in the plain register, not deadpan, because it's a death.)

## Quick Checklist

For each candidate story:
- [ ] Can you state who's affected and how, in one sentence?
- [ ] Can you state the story's hook — the image, irony, or tension — in one sentence, separate from the impact?
- [ ] If you can only answer one of the two above, is the story strong enough on that single axis to justify running anyway (rare — most single-axis stories should be cut)?
- [ ] Across the drop's three stories, is there real range — not three of the same register?
- [ ] Would you personally text this to a friend whose taste you respect, unprompted?

## Worked Examples

**Missouri congressional map ruling** — Matters: yes, real electoral consequence. Has a story: no — no person, no image, no irony, just a procedural outcome. **Likely cut**, or run only if no stronger alternative exists that drop and the stakes are high enough to justify the dry read.

**Pope prioritizes church duties over his own health** — Matters: moderate — affects how one institution is run. Has a story: strong — a specific, human, quietly dramatic choice. **Runs.**

**Fatal shooting over a spice-level argument** — Matters: real, but localized (affects a family, not a broader public). Has a story: very strong — stark juxtaposition between trivial cause and severe outcome. **Would pass selection**, but gets written in the plain/exception register per the voice doc, not deadpan, because it's a death.

## The Drop Category Framework
Each drop has its own identity, with three subcategories guiding what belongs in it. This has been governing Select's actual behavior for a while now but was never formally written down until this pass.

**Morning — Truth & Clarity:** global stability (conflicts, diplomacy, geopolitical shifts), public systems (healthcare, education, infrastructure, policy), economic signals (inflation, jobs, markets, cost-of-living).

**Afternoon — People & Progress:** breakthroughs, community wins, innovation, resilience, positive cultural shifts, stories of people building, creating, or improving something. This keeps the human element Afternoon always had, but shifts the energy from harm toward growth — meaningful and human without being depressing or corny.

**The valence rule — what actually differentiates this from Late Night:** both drops can touch culture, sports, entertainment, or internet discourse. The difference isn't subject matter, it's tone requirement. Late Night has no positivity filter — a lawsuit over workplace discrimination or a TV episode reworked after a national tragedy both fit Late Night's reflective, any-valence "culture pulse." Afternoon specifically requires a growth or resilience angle — the story has to be about something getting built, healed, won, or improved. If a candidate story's dominant feeling is wrongdoing, loss, or failure with no real resolution, it belongs in Morning or Late Night if it fits their identity, not Afternoon.

**Why the default can stay light: the voice system already handles weight.** This isn't an avoidance strategy — the Tribute Register, discourse-neutral framing, and the "insight stays, ornamentation goes" rule already exist specifically to let real weight be covered with genuine class. Afternoon's default doesn't need to also carry that burden, which is what the Major Story Override below is for.

**Late Night — Discernment & Reflection:** culture pulse (sports, entertainment, internet discourse, public sentiment), ethical tension (dilemmas, debates, moral questions shaping society), long-arc stories (climate, technology, generational change, future-shaping trends).

## Major Story Override
Applies across all three drops, not just Afternoon. When a story is significant enough that any serious news outlet would be running it — the Clancy bar, not routine crime or corruption — it overrides normal category fit entirely and runs in whichever drop's timing makes sense, regardless of whether it matches that drop's usual identity.

This is how real newsrooms actually work: regular beats most days, breaking exceptions when something genuinely warrants it. Afternoon isn't "the accountability drop" or "the culture drop" — it's People & Progress by default, with room to carry something like Clancy when it actually happens. The bar is deliberately high: this is for the rare, unmissable case, not a loophole back into routine hard news.

## Fallback Rule: When a Drop's Category Has No Strong Candidate
This governs the Morning/Afternoon/Late Night category framework (Truth & Clarity / Justice & Compassion / Discernment & Reflection), which sits alongside the Two-Question Test above. The relationship between the two: the **Two-Question Test governs whether a story gets picked at all**; this fallback governs **which drop a story lands in** once a category comes up dry for the day. There is one selection standard, not two — this fallback doesn't introduce a separate filter, it just tells you where to route a story that already passed the Two-Question Test but doesn't fit its category that day.

**The rule:** if a category has no strong candidate, select the closest adjacent story that already passes the Two-Question Test, and categorize it by **tone, not topic**:
- Reads as clarity → Morning
- Reads as impact → Afternoon
- Reads as reflection → Late Night

**Absolute precedence:** never break the 3-story structure. Always break the category first. The product's rhythm matters more than strict categorical purity.

**One-sentence version, reusable anywhere:** *Categories are flexible, the Two-Question Test is fixed. If a category is empty, choose the story that best fits the time-of-day tone while still passing that test.*

## Open Question Worth Deciding Later
How much does a story's "matters" score need to clear on its own before "has a story" can't save it? Right now this doc treats the two axes as independent judgment calls, not a formula — worth revisiting once you've run this against a few weeks of real candidate stories and can see where the edge cases actually cluster.
